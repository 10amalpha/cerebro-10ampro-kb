Anthropic shares gained 17 per cent on their first day of trading in what was the biggest initial public offering in history. But amid Friday’s rout of tech stocks, the AI company’s stock came within five cents of its $83 offer price before closing at $88.91, down 8.5 per cent. For some investors, that may raise questions about the management of the IPO post debut.


Clearly, the scale of the tech market bloodbath suggests there might be a broader questioning of the dominant AI trade, something the Anthropic flotation might have contributed to. But there is also the issue of whether the underwriters of the IPO were overly hasty in exercising the full overallotment option or “greenshoe”.

What is the greenshoe? On an IPO, underwriters typically sell investors 15 per cent more shares than the base offering, creating a short position. If the share price rises, they cover it by exercising an option to buy additional shares from the issuer at the IPO price. If the shares fall, they buy stock in the market to cover the short, providing support for the price. The option normally lasts 30 days.

Viewed in isolation, the decision here to exercise the overallotment option after just one day of trading made sense. Anthropic closed the first day at $97.17, safely above the $83 IPO price. Such a solid debut normally means there’s little need to keep dry powder for stabilisation. Moreover, exercising the greenshoe generated another $12.2bn of proceeds for the company.

But in this case, several circumstances have prompted questions as to whether it might have been better to wait.


First, Anthropic had already increased the offering size by 19 per cent. It had also priced the shares at $83, well above the original $70 to $78 range. The larger offering and higher price signalled strong investor appetite. But there is a difference between capitalising on vigorous demand and pushing the market to see how much stock it can absorb.

Second, there was already a considerable amount of “loose” stock supply. Up to 315mn shares held by non-founder directors, officers, employees and consultants — worth about $26bn at IPO price — were eligible for sale in the market from day one.

Third, market conditions were deteriorating even before Friday’s sell-off. By the time Anthropic went public, the Nasdaq 100 had lost about 12 per cent since peaking in October, while US stocks fell 2 per cent on IPO day. In addition, after initially spiking to $115, Anthropic shares declined throughout the session and continued to fall in after-hours trading on Thursday.

Against that backdrop, exercising the greenshoe so quickly — a decision that would normally be uncontroversial — seems somewhat opportunistic. It also sends an unhelpful signal about Anthropic’s appetite for cash.

The purpose of a greenshoe is to enable the banks to stabilise the share price after a large injection of new supply, not to raise additional proceeds. The short position created by overallotting shares gives the lead bank the ability to support the stock if it falls back to or below the IPO price.

In this way, the greenshoe is a form of insurance for an orderly after-market rather than a way of squeezing out the last dollar. Once the banks exercise the option, they can no longer support the shares by buying in the market to close out their short position if the stock weakens in subsequent sessions.

Why then didn’t Anthropic’s underwriters wait a few days just to make sure the stock settled into a stable pattern?

Let’s dismiss one motivation: Morgan Stanley, which had responsibility for stabilisation, had no direct financial incentive to exercise the greenshoe. As with SpaceX’s IPO, the prospectus makes explicit that the underwriters receive no commission on shares sold pursuant to the overallotment option. All of the extra $12.2bn raised goes into Anthropic’s coffers.

Legally, the underwriters have sole discretion to exercise the greenshoe. But in my experience, the lead manager would invariably consult with the company and try to accommodate its wishes. It’s unimaginable for a bank to act unilaterally, regardless of the legal position. It seems likely, therefore, that Anthropic approved, or even encouraged, the early exercise.

The greenshoe exercise highlights the mixed signals sent by the Anthropic IPO. Pricing above the range conveyed strength and confidence. But for some investors, increasing the offering size smacked of opportunism. And exercising the greenshoe after just one day — despite uncertain market conditions and the potential supply of insider stock — reinforces that impression. It suggests a desire to maximise fundraising while the capital markets are open.

There’s a fine line between raising the capital you need and seizing whatever is on offer. With so much riding on the Anthropic flotation, it would be a shame if the apparent dash for more cash overshadows what has otherwise been a textbook execution.
