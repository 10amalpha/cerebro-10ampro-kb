**Hernán:** CPI Roulette Is Not Monetary Policy

Wall Street is addicted to the next CPI print. Every month, the same spectacle: analysts arguing over a tenth of a percentage point as if it reveals the whole economy. Yet they rarely ask the obvious question: How far above r* (the neutral rate) is the federal funds rate already?

Why Fed is already too tight!!

And how much of the Fed’s prior tightening is still moving through the system?

Key point: the Fed should get the FFR to r* now. Yes they should cut 100 bps.

Monetary policy has lags. Everybody knows it. But Wall Street ignores them because admitting the truth wrecks the story it wants to tell: that the Fed can steer a $30T economy with neat 25bps adjustments. It cannot.

Listen to Fed officials imply that they can fine tune the US economy with a 25 bps cut is comical.

The policy set today is not fully felt next month. It can take 12 to 24 months to work through housing, credit, investment, and jobs.

And why is the supply side always treated as an afterthought? A Fed that is too tight does not just restrain demand. It blocks supply from adjusting.

Look at housing. Higher rates choke off construction finance, make development less viable, trap homeowners in low-rate mortgages, and reduce listings. The result is fewer homes available and then the same people cite stubborn shelter costs as proof that rates must remain high.

That is circular policy logic!

The broader mistake is treating every increase in the price level as “inflation.” An energy spike, tariff, supply disruption, or relative-price shift is not necessarily a monetary inflation problem. Yet the response is always the same: hike rates.

To be clear: a change in the relative price level is not inflation!!!!

Apparently, higher borrowing costs can drill for oil, build homes, fix supply chains, and erase fiscal excess.

And why do pundits so often ignore market-based inflation expectations, including breakevens, when they contradict the next-CPI-print panic?

Then come the familiar warnings about second- and third-order effects, wage-price spirals, unanchored expectations, and feedback loops that are always supposedly around the corner but rarely arrive. Look at the research, Supply Shocks dissipate left to their own devices!!

This is not serious analysis. It is CPI roulette, dressed up in models and false precision. They ignore lags, dismiss supply, disregard market prices, and worship flawed data, then call the consequences “unexpected.”
